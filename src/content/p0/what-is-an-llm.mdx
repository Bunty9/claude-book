At its heart, a large language model does one surprisingly simple thing: given some text, it predicts what text should come next. That's it. Everything else — answering questions, writing code, explaining concepts, engaging in conversation — emerges from doing that one thing extremely well, at enormous scale, over enormous amounts of training data.

Understanding that core mechanic is the foundation for using Claude effectively. Once it clicks, a lot of Claude's behaviour — both its strengths and its limitations — starts to make intuitive sense.

## Tokens: the atoms of language

LLMs don't read text the way you do, word by word. They work with **tokens** — sub-word fragments from a vocabulary built by analyzing a large text corpus before training begins. The tokenizer is a separate preprocessing step, distinct from learning the model's weights. Common short words are usually single tokens. Longer or rarer words often get split: "tokenization" might become `token`, `ization`. Punctuation, whitespace, and numbers each have their own patterns.

A rough rule of thumb: one token is about ¾ of an English word, or roughly 4 characters.

Token counts matter for two practical reasons. First, every API call is priced per token (input and output separately). Second, there is a hard ceiling on how many tokens a single interaction can involve — the **context window**.

## Context: everything the model can see

The context window is the model's entire working memory for a conversation. It contains the system prompt, every message in the conversation so far, any documents you've pasted in, and the outputs of any tools the model called. Nothing outside the context window exists for the model — it cannot look it up, reference it, or be influenced by it.

Modern Claude models have very large context windows (hundreds of thousands of tokens), but they are still finite. When a conversation grows long enough to approach the limit, older content is compacted or summarised to make room. This is why very long sessions can lose fidelity on details mentioned hours ago.

Practically: if something matters, make sure it is in the context. Don't assume Claude "remembers" something from a previous session — each new conversation starts with a blank context.

## Training vs. inference: learned once, runs by predicting

A language model is built in two distinct phases.

**Training** happens once (or infrequently). The model is exposed to enormous volumes of text, and through a process of repeated prediction and correction, it learns a compressed statistical model of language and knowledge. This is where Claude acquired its understanding of grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, and how to be helpful. Training is expensive and slow — it runs on thousands of GPUs for weeks or months.

**Inference** is what happens every time you send a message. The trained model, with its fixed weights, takes your context and predicts the next token. Then the next. Then the next. There is no further learning happening during this process — the model is not updating itself based on your conversation. It is, in a precise sense, running a very sophisticated autocomplete.

This distinction matters: Claude cannot learn new facts during a conversation. Its knowledge has a cutoff date from training. What it *can* do is reason carefully over information you provide in context — which is why grounding Claude in fresh documents, search results, or tool outputs is so powerful.

## Why Claude can be confidently wrong

This is the most important thing to understand about any LLM: it predicts plausible text, not verified truth.

When Claude generates a response, it is producing text that fits the pattern of what a correct, helpful answer looks like — based on what it learned during training. Most of the time, plausible and correct are the same thing. But sometimes they diverge. The model may produce a date, a name, a citation, or a code snippet that sounds authoritative and turns out to be wrong. This is called **hallucination**, and it is not a bug in the traditional sense — it is a fundamental consequence of how prediction works.

The model has no internal "fact-checker" separate from the prediction process. It does not experience uncertainty the way you do. It can be asked to express uncertainty, and it often does so appropriately, but the underlying mechanism is still prediction.

<Callout type="warning" title="Verify anything high-stakes">
For decisions that matter — medical, legal, financial, or factual claims you'll share publicly — treat Claude's output as a knowledgeable first draft, not a ground truth. Ask Claude to reason through its answer, check sources independently, and use tool calls that return real data where possible.
</Callout>

## So what does this mean for using Claude?

The chapters ahead explore this in depth, but here is the short version:

Claude is most powerful when you treat it as a capable reasoning partner operating over information you supply — not as an oracle that independently knows the truth. Give it clear context. Tell it what role to play and what outcome you want. Ground it in real data when accuracy matters. Use its extended thinking and tool-calling capabilities to catch its own errors.

The rest of this book is about how to do exactly that — across prompting, tool use, multi-step agents, and the full API surface. Understanding the token-predicting machine underneath everything else is where that journey begins.
